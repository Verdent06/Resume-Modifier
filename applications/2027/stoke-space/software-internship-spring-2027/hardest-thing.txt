WHAT IS THE HARDEST THING YOU HAVE DONE? WHAT DID YOU LEARN FROM IT?

The hardest thing I have done is build Dadei from nothing—a voice assistant that listens to ambient conversation, proposes calendar, email, and task actions, and refuses to execute any of them until a human explicitly approves.

I started because I was frustrated with assistants that either did nothing useful or acted too eagerly. The product idea was simple to describe and brutal to implement: real-time transcription, speaker attribution in a room with multiple people and multiple microphones, context retrieval good enough to propose the right meeting or follow-up, and an orchestration layer that survives Redis outages without silently dying. No course syllabus covered that end-to-end stack.

The first wall was latency. Users will not tolerate an assistant that feels sluggish. I split speech recognition into two paths: a streaming Deepgram connection for live commands and a batched Whisper path for bulk ambient transcription. The React client streams microphone audio over a WebSocket with client-side Web Audio processing; captions need to arrive in under 300 milliseconds or the UI feels broken. That constraint rippled backward through every design choice—what runs in the request path, what gets pushed to background workers, what gets cached.

The second wall was identity in the same room. When two phones are listening, you get duplicate transcripts and duplicated proposals. I built two arbitration layers: an utterance-owner election for live capture and a 400 millisecond windowed quality election on signal-to-noise ratio, clarity, and cadence. It cut duplicate transcription by roughly half. Speaker ID without labeled training data was another rabbit hole: an EMA voice centroid, a bounded prototype bank, floor-and-margin gating, abstaining into a new identity when uncertain. Getting to 96% correct attribution felt less like a machine-learning triumph and more like weeks of listening to edge cases I had not imagined.

The third wall—and the one that changed how I think about systems—was reliability. I decoupled workers from the FastAPI service using Redis Streams with consumer groups, dead-letter routing after five retries, and stale-claim recovery. At-least-once delivery was non-negotiable; pub/sub had already lost messages in testing. Then production humbled me: the worker queue would sometimes exit with code zero when Redis dropped. No stack trace, no crash—just gone. I root-caused it, added a health check that detects the condition, and wrapped the backend in a three-layer restart running two Python services from one Docker image. That week taught me more than any textbook chapter on distributed systems: silent failure is worse than loud failure, especially when an LLM is downstream proposing real-world actions.

Through all of that I held one product invariant: every irreversible side effect routes through a single approval chokepoint with a cancelable countdown. Calendar writes, emails, tasks—one auditable seam between the model and the outside world. That decision forced discipline. I could not hide behind "the model decided." I had to build retrieval that grounds proposals under a fixed token budget—hybrid pgvector search fusing vector similarity, lexical overlap, recency, and participant overlap—and a client that makes approval and cancellation obvious.

What I learned:

First, scope to the failure that would destroy trust, not the feature that would look impressive in a demo. Approval, delivery guarantees, and recovery came before polish.

Second, ownership means validating on the thing that actually breaks. For Dadei that was Redis drops and duplicate microphones; at Stoke I expect it to be timing on a test stand or a race in telemetry ingestion. I want to find those seams early.

Third, hard problems are rarely one clever algorithm. They are dozens of mediocre bottlenecks chained together. Progress came from reducing the system until I could reason about one layer at a time, then putting it back together and watching it fail again.

Fourth, I work best when the problem is open-ended and the metric is whether the system survives contact with reality. That is why I am applying to Vehicle Software at Stoke. Building software that must run on bench testers and flight hardware is the same muscle I have been training—just with stakes I care about deeply.

I am not finished with Dadei; complex systems are never finished. But shipping it taught me how to stay focused, move fast, self-direct, and learn on the fly—which is exactly what the internship description asks for. I want to bring that posture to Nova.
